Ports

Today, a port is no longer just a place to dock.

Consider Hambantota, Sri Lanka.
(or) Start at the southern tip of Sri Lanka.

Why spend over a billion dollars on a quiet shoreline when Colombo—Sri Lanka’s bustling commercial hub—is just hours away?

Hambantota is one of the busiest port in the world, where it can take approximately 200 to 300 vessels pass this lane daily (including roughly 4,500 oil tankers), carrying nearly half of the world's maritime trade.

In 2008, China loaned Sri Lanka over $1.1 billion to build a deep-water port in Hambantota—framed as a partnership for regional development.

The port sits just north of the East–West shipping lane connecting the Strait of Malacca to the Suez Canal.

Global shipping lanes converge around the southern tip of Sri Lanka, whoever controls or operates out of Hambantota can monitor, influence, or potentially disrupt traffic moving between the world's real choke points.

Hambantota is not an anomaly. Over the past two decades, Chinese state-backed firms have acquired stakes in nearly 100 ports worldwide.
